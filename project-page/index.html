Abstract

Anatomical liver resection (ALR) surgery is the most important treatment for liver cancer, yet preoperative planning demands complex, multi-stage clinical reasoning under competing safety constraints. Current 2D desktop tools are not well equipped to support this process, exhibiting three fundamental limitations: reliance on monolithic interfaces that fail to adapt to the distinct cognitive demands of each planning stage; a perceptual bottleneck caused by limited anatomical spatial representation and missing plane-vessel intersection visualization; and an attention bottleneck stemming from fragmented critical safety criteria display across separate views.

Read the full abstractShow less

We present LiverPlan, a stage-adaptive immersive visual analytics framework for ALR planning, grounded in an 8-month collaboration with two expert hepatobiliary surgeons. Decomposing the surgical planning process into three sequential yet cognitively distinct stages, LiverPlan externalizes the cognitive demand of each stage via tailored techniques: (1) context-preserving focus and hue-preserving rendering for anatomical discovery; (2) direct 3D resection plane manipulation coupled with real-time, embedded visual feedback on critical safety criteria during plan refinement; (3) and explicit plane-vessel intersection visualization for anticipatory surgery preparation.

A within-subjects study with eight hepatobiliary surgeons against a desktop baseline shows large-effect-size improvements in task completion time, perceived cognitive workload, and system usability on controlled planning tasks. Moreover, our study reveals broader insights: LiverPlan reduces cognitive burden and encourages a shift in surgeons from merely satisfying safety criteria to actively optimizing them, suggesting that explicit visualization of spatial relationships lowers the cognitive barrier to complex surgical planning.

Method

Anatomical liver resection planning moves from understanding complex anatomy to balancing safety criteria and preparing for surgery. LiverPlan adapts visualization and interaction to the cognitive demands of each stage.

1

Stage 1: Tumor-Vessel Spatial Analysis

Anatomical discovery to identify candidate target liver segments for resection.

G1Externalize Spatial Perception R1Context-Preserving Focus R2Maintain Color Fidelity

Key contribution

Enhanced visualization of tumor-vessel spatial relationships.

  1. (a) Macroscopic representationsWhole-liver and liver-segment views support inspection of tumor invasion and individual segment morphology.
  2. (b) Microscopic representationsAdjustable vessel thickness exposes deep tumor-vessel relationships while preserving vascular context.
  3. (c) Hue-preserving renderingSelective desaturation of overlapping outer liver surfaces preserves the diagnostic colors of internal vessels and tumors.

Stage 2: Interactive Resection Planning

Surgeons grab, move, and rotate resection planes directly in 3D to refine the plan against safety criteria.

G2Streamline Multi-Constraint Planning R3Direct Spatial Interaction R4Embedded Visual Feedback

Key contribution

Real-time, embedded visual feedback on critical safety criteria.

Constraint status stays within the focused 3D view during resection plane manipulation, so surgeons can compare safety criteria and trade them off without shifting attention.

  • FLRFunctional Liver RemnantA semi-transparent, color-coded remnant outline and a floating numerical indicator.
  • RMResection MarginA connector line with a disk indicator or a surface distance map on the active resection plane.
  • TSCRTarget Segment Complete RemovalDynamic contours indicate whether a resection plane intersects a target segment, warning of incomplete removal.

Stage 3: Plane-Vessel Intersection Analysis

Surgery preparation: understand the vascular implications of the finalized plan.

G3Anticipatory Mental Model R5Explicit Intersection Visualization

Key contribution

Enhanced spatial understanding of the resection plan.

Focus+Context plane-vessel intersection visualization shows which portions of the vasculature are resected or preserved, where resection planes intersect vessels, and the caliber of the intersected branches.

  • (b) Context-aware skeletonized vessel displayResected branches appear in desaturated gray, while preserved branches retain their diagnostic hues.

Study design

Each participant completed the full planning workflow, Stage 1 to Stage 3, for every case.

Participants
8 hepatobiliary surgeons from two partner hospitals: 3 attending and 5 resident surgeons, mean age 27.88 years (SD 3.23).
Baseline
3D Slicer, configured to reproduce the task-relevant functions of the commercial planning software used by the collaborating surgeons.
Cases & order
Four clinical cases at two complexity levels, split into two case groups counterbalanced across platforms. Half of the participants used LiverPlan first.
Measures & analysis
Task time, NASA-TLX, confidence, Likert ratings, SUS, and interviews. Paired t-tests, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, Cohen’s d.

Beyond the metrics

Two broader insights from the interviews.

Finding 1

Reduced cognitive burden unlocks deeper planning engagement.

Lower visualization and interaction costs encouraged surgeons to further check and refine, shifting them from merely satisfying constraints to optimizing them.

“Perhaps because this system provides more intuitive visual feedback, especially the visualization of resection margins, it made my evaluation more detailed, which actually took longer.” P1

Finding 2

Explicit visual representations make spatial reasoning more accessible.

Complex spatial relationships become directly observable rather than mentally reconstructed, which was particularly evident among less experienced participants.

“The intuitive visual representation makes it much easier to understand the spatial relationships, which would be incredibly valuable for young surgeons and medical students who are still developing their spatial reasoning skills.” P4
